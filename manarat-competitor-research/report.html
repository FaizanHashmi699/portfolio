Manarat Foundation is third of six by income in its local market — not second — and it was overtaken in the year just reported.

The Olton Project, a charity registered in 2019 and based in Olton, inside Manarat's own stated catchment, raised £496,482 in the year to 31 March 2025. Manarat raised £394,135 in the identical financial year — down 13% from £452,950.

The Olton Project's core offer is Qur'an and Islamic studies for ages 4–16, running every day of the week, streamed by ability, in small classes with qualified teachers, sold through a dedicated academy website. That is Manarat's core programme, positioned better and marketed harder by an organisation less than half its age.

The underlying position is still strong. Income roughly tripled between 2021 and 2024. The building holds a thousand people. The three-year Hifz programme is substantively better than what beat it. And five years of surpluses have accumulated around £660,600 in reserves.

But three structural gaps now have a cost attached: the website cannot take a donation; the education offer has no shop window; and £660k of reserves has no public name on it, so it raises nothing and explains nothing.

Demand is real. Muslims were 5.3% of Solihull's population at the 2021 Census, up from 2.5% in 2011 — more than double in a decade. Birmingham overall stands at 29.9%, up 8.0 points, the largest rise of any religious group in the city.

Supply grew faster. Manarat opened in 2012 as the first purpose-established masjid for Sheldon and Solihull. At least five organisations now serve that same catchment: The Olton Project (2019), Solihull Central Masjid (2016) — which now markets itself as the first and only masjid in Solihull with a full-time Imam — Deen Central operating as The Hub, Solihull Islamic Education Academy, and Manarat itself.

Manarat's founding advantage was being first into an underserved area. That advantage has expired. The market is still growing; Manarat's share of it is not.

The Olton Project — the one that overtook you

Registered as a CIO in June 2019, based on Kineton Green Road in Olton, with 7 trustees and 11 volunteers. Income £496,482 against expenditure £256,489 in the year to 31 March 2025 — the same year Manarat raised £394,135.

TOP Academy is the engine: Qur'an and Islamic studies for ages 4–16, lessons every day of the week, weekday and weekend classes streamed by ability, a low teacher-to-pupil ratio, and teachers drawn largely from child-facing professions. It is framed as equipping children "to practise their religion in modern Britain," and it has its own domain. Around it sit spiritual gatherings, language classes, weekly coffee mornings, educational excursions, monthly movie nights, guest speakers, social functions, and food and equipment delivery to vulnerable local people.

TOP is positioned on exactly what parents actually choose on — daily availability, ability streaming, small classes, qualified staff — and it is sold on a site of its own. Manarat's three-year Hifz programme is a stronger offer and is effectively invisible next to it.

Jami Mosque & Islamic Centre — the incumbent

Same arterial road, three miles west, capacity 2,000, a fifty-year head start — and income that has more than doubled since 2021: £818k, £1.39m, £1.34m, £1.90m.

The number that matters: only 19% of income comes from donations. Rental property and education fees carry it. And its donation income — £362,430 — is below Manarat's total income. Manarat is competitive on fundraising; it is not competitive on diversification.

Solihull Central Masjid — the rising claim

Established 2016 on Warwick Road. It filed a nil return for 2024, so its published £0 tells you nothing. It competes on convenience — town centre, M42/M40/M6 — monetises the building through catering equipment hire, and markets itself as the first and only masjid in Solihull with a full-time Imam. That claim is aimed squarely at the ground Manarat should own.

Solihull Islamic Education Academy — the quiet overlap

£156,496 income, 86.3% spent. Sunday supplementary school, mother-tongue languages beyond Arabic — Bangla and Urdu — weekly adult Islamic education, talks on Islam in schools, and weekly badminton and football clubs for teenagers. Not a masjid, but direct competition for the same families' children.

Deen Central, operating as The Hub — the cautionary case

Revision 1 treated Deen Central and The Hub as two organisations. They are one, at 5–9 Hermitage Road, Solihull. It has publicly stated its donor income dropped by almost 100% while daily overheads continued.

Even so, it runs daily and Jumu'ah prayers, Quran classes for children and adults, a bereavement service, community events, function rooms for hire, a live prayer-times page and a newsletter — three of which Manarat does not have, from a far weaker financial base.

Green Lane Masjid — the benchmark

£5.38m income, 93.9% deployed, and growth every year for five years: £3.61m → £4.69m → £4.80m → £5.00m → £5.38m. A foodbank running eight years, Celebrate Eid at 60,000–80,000 attendees, and an international wing that has raised over £7m in six years.

The transferable lesson is not scale. It is that Green Lane never asks for a donation. It asks for a named project, monthly, at a daily price — "£20 a month, 67p a day" — with every appeal on a page of its own. And its 94% spend ratio is itself the fundraising argument.

Manarat raised £394,135 in the identical financial year. TOP's core offer is Qur'an and Islamic studies for ages 4–16, running every day of the week, streamed by ability, in small classes, sold through a dedicated academy website. F1
Income fell for the first time in five years
Critical
£128.9k → £210.1k → £330.4k → £453.0k → £394.1k. A 13% drop, with expenditure still rising from £232.9k to £239.2k. One down year is not a trend — but it is the first, and it landed in the same year a local rival out-raised the organisation.
F2
The website cannot take a donation
High
Published routes are cheque and in person. Every direct competitor with a live site except SIEA takes card payments; The Olton Project takes them through JustGiving. Cash-and-cheque giving structurally excludes under-40 donors and makes Ramadan night-giving — the highest-intent moment in the Islamic giving calendar — almost impossible to capture.
F3
Prayer times are a six-year-old PDF
High
The timetable on the site was uploaded in July 2020. Prayer times are the highest-frequency reason a local Muslim opens a masjid website, and that recurring traffic currently goes to MosquePay, The Masjid App and MosqueFinder. Jami, Deen Central and Green Lane all host live timetables themselves.
F4
£660k has accumulated with no public name on it
High
Five years of surpluses total roughly £660,600. The 61% spend ratio is not itself the problem — Jami runs 65.8% and Olton 51.7%, so reserve-building is normal here. The problem is that Manarat's reserve is the only one with no named project attached. Green Lane has its Expansion Project; Jami is visibly building property and a school. An unnamed reserve raises nothing and explains nothing.
F5
The education offer has no shop window
High
The Olton Project sells its academy on its own domain; SIEA has a dedicated site. Manarat's three-year Hifz programme, Arabic course and Islamic studies — substantively a stronger offer than either — live inside one undifferentiated /services/ page with no fees, no timetable, no age ranges and no enquiry form. Parents choosing a madrasah cannot compare Manarat without phoning.
F6
Single-source income concentration
Medium
Manarat appears almost entirely donation-funded and is the only organisation in the set with no earned-income line. Jami takes 19% from donations and carries the rest on rental and school fees; Solihull Central hires out catering equipment; Deen Central hires out rooms; TOP and SIEA charge course fees.
F7
The founding advantage has expired
Medium
Manarat opened in 2012 as the first purpose-established masjid for Sheldon and Solihull. At least five organisations now serve that catchment. Demand is genuinely growing — Solihull's Muslim population went from 2.5% to 5.3% between the 2011 and 2021 Censuses — but supply has grown faster, and Solihull Central now claims to be the first and only Solihull masjid with a full-time Imam.
F8
No funeral or bereavement service
Medium
Jami has run funerals since 1995, Green Lane offers them, and Deen Central runs a bereavement service from a much weaker financial base. A bereaved family that has to go elsewhere at the hardest moment of their lives tends to keep going there afterwards. This is the highest-loyalty service in the sector.
F9
No visible women's or youth programme
Medium
Jami runs JMIC Muslimah with its own site section; SIEA runs weekly badminton and football; TOP runs coffee mornings, excursions and movie nights. Manarat's public offer — prayers, Quran, Arabic, Nikah — reaches men and children, but not women or teenagers as distinct constituencies.
F10
Discoverability is rented, not owned
Low
MosquePay, The Masjid App, MosqueFinder and Giving is Great own the recurring visit and the donor relationship. Manarat also has no newsletter, so there is no owned channel to a single supporter — something Deen Central maintains even while financially distressed.
F11
The strongest differentiator is unstated
Low
Founded by scholars, the first purpose-established masjid for Sheldon and Solihull, and an explicit anti-extremism position. That last one is rare, publicly valuable, and absent from how the site sells itself — while Solihull Central actively markets its full-time Imam.

Method, sources and gaps

What this research rests on — and what it doesn't

Gaps — limits of the research environment, not of the market

No live page crawls. Outbound HTTPS to these domains is blocked by the research environment's egress proxy (403 on CONNECT, re-tested this round). Site structure is evidenced by known URLs from the search index, so page inventories are a floor, not a ceiling.
No quantitative SEO data. The connected Ahrefs plan does not include Site Explorer, and the Semrush account has no API units remaining. There are no traffic, domain-rating or keyword figures anywhere in this research — every quantitative claim is financial or census-based.
Solihull Central Masjid filed a nil return for the year to 31 December 2024, so its published £0 does not reflect real activity. It is likely a stronger competitor than its figures suggest.
