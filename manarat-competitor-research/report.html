Against a 1973 incumbent next door

Manarat Foundation is the second-largest organisation by income in its immediate local market — £394,135 in the year to 31 March 2025, behind only Jami Mosque & Islamic Centre and well ahead of every other Solihull-area competitor. For an organisation founded in 2012, competing against an institution that has been on the same road since 1973, that is a genuinely strong position.

The vulnerabilities are not about size. They are three specific things:

The website cannot take a donation. Published giving routes are cheque and in person. Every direct competitor except the education academy takes card payments.

The prayer timetable is a PDF from July 2020. The highest-frequency reason to visit a masjid website has been handed to third-party aggregators.

Only 61% of income is being spent. A ~£156k annual surplus is accumulating with no publicly named project attached to it.

All three are unusually cheap to fix relative to their impact, and the first two are website work rather than organisational change.

Jami Mosque & Islamic Centre — the real rival

Same arterial road, about three miles west, with a fifty-year head start on donor relationships and a capacity of 2,000 across two halls. It runs an evening maktab from 1975, a Hifz madrasa from 1983, a full-time Darul Uloom boys' secondary school from 1985, and funeral services from 1995.

But the number that matters is this: only 19% of its £1.93m income comes from donations. Rental property and education fees carry the organisation. And its donation income — £362,430 — is slightly below Manarat's total income. Manarat is already competitive on fundraising. It is not competitive on diversification.

Solihull Central Masjid — the rising claim

Established 2016 on Warwick Road, and the nearest masjid inside Solihull proper. It competes on convenience rather than scholarship — town-centre proximity, motorway access via the M42, M40 and M6 — and monetises its building through catering equipment hire. It filed a nil return for 2024, so its published £0 tells you nothing; it is almost certainly a stronger competitor than the register suggests. As it matures, Manarat's claim to be the masjid for Sheldon and Solihull gets harder to hold.

Solihull Islamic Education Academy — the quiet overlap

Not a masjid, so no competition for prayer footfall — but direct competition for the same families' children and their weekend education spend, which is Manarat's core programme. It teaches mother-tongue languages beyond Arabic — Bangla and Urdu — and runs weekly badminton and football clubs for teenagers. Both reach families that a prayer-and-Quran-only offer does not.

Deen Central — the cautionary case

A Solihull organisation with an overlapping education-and-community pitch that has publicly stated its donor income dropped by almost 100% while daily masjid overheads continued. That is a live demonstration of the risk in donation-only funding against a fixed building — the exact shape of Manarat's own income. It may be worth approaching as a partner rather than treating as a rival.

Green Lane Masjid — the benchmark

£5.38m income, 94% of it deployed, and a fundraising machine: a foodbank running eight years, Celebrate Eid at 60,000–80,000 attendees, and an international wing that has raised over £7m in six years. Roughly 13.6× Manarat's income, with donation income alone about 12× Manarat's total.

The transferable lesson is not scale. It is that Green Lane never asks for a donation. It asks for a named project, monthly, at a daily price — "£20 a month, 67p a day" — with every appeal on a page of its own. And its 94% spend ratio is itself the fundraising argument.

Findings

Nine, by severity · IDs referenced by the recommendations
F1
The website cannot take a donation
High
Published routes are cheque and in person. Cash-and-cheque giving structurally excludes under-40 donors and makes Ramadan night-giving — the highest-intent moment in the Islamic giving calendar — almost impossible to capture. Online giving is currently intermediated by MosquePay.
F2
Prayer times are a six-year-old PDF
High
The timetable on the site was uploaded in July 2020. Prayer times are the highest-frequency reason a local Muslim opens a masjid website, and that recurring traffic currently goes to MosquePay, The Masjid App and MosqueFinder instead. Jami, Deen Central and Green Lane all host live timetables themselves.
F3
Only 61% of income is being spent
High
£394,135 in, £239,198 out, a ~£156k surplus in one year. Green Lane deploys 94%, the education academy 86%. Either Manarat is building toward a capital project — in which case that project is not named anywhere public and is therefore raising nothing — or it is under-deploying. Both readings have the same fix: name the project.
F4
Single-source income concentration
Medium
Manarat appears almost entirely donation-funded. Jami takes 19% from donations; Solihull Central hires out catering equipment; the academy charges fees. Deen Central, donation-only, has reported a near-total collapse in donor income. Manarat has a 1,000-capacity building and a large car park with no visible earned-income line.
F5
Thin site, no per-service pages
Medium
Roughly five top-level pages against Jami's service tree and Green Lane's project-per-page structure. The Hifz programme, Arabic course and combined Islamic-plus-civil marriage registration each justify their own page, and each is a distinct search entry point currently collapsed into one.
F6
No funeral or janazah service
Medium
Jami has run funerals since 1995; Green Lane offers them. A bereaved family that travels to Small Heath at the hardest moment of their lives tends to keep going there afterwards. This is the highest-loyalty service in the sector and Manarat does not appear to offer it.
F7
No visible women's or youth programme
Medium
Jami runs a dedicated Muslimah section; the academy runs weekly badminton and football. Manarat's public offer — prayers, Quran, Arabic, Nikah — reaches men and children, but not women or teenagers as distinct constituencies.
F8
Discoverability is rented, not owned
Low
MosquePay, The Masjid App, MosqueFinder and Giving is Great own the recurring visit and the donor relationship. Fine as acquisition channels; a problem as the primary one.
F9
The strongest differentiator is unstated
Low
Founded by scholars, the first purpose-established masjid for Sheldon and Solihull, and an explicit anti-extremism position. That last one is rare, publicly valuable, and absent from how the site sells itself. Solihull Central competes on parking and motorway access; Manarat can compete on scholarship and civic trust.

Method, sources and gaps

What this research rests on — and what it doesn't

Sources. Charity Commission for England and Wales (Register of Charities) for all financial and governance figures; Companies House; organisation websites surfaced via the search index; and the MosquePay, The Masjid App, TotalGiving and CharityChoice donation platforms.

Gaps — limits of the research environment, not of the market

No live page crawls. Outbound HTTPS to these domains was blocked by the research environment's egress proxy. Site structure is evidenced by known URLs from the search index rather than a full crawl, so page inventories are a floor, not a ceiling.
No quantitative SEO data. The connected Ahrefs plan does not include Site Explorer, and the Semrush account has no API units remaining. There are no traffic, domain-rating or keyword figures anywhere in this research — every quantitative claim here is financial, from the Charity Commission.
